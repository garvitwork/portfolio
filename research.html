Mechanistic Interpretability · GPT-2 small

The Negation Circuit in GPT-2 Small

GPT-2 flips its sentiment prediction under negation — "the film was really good" becomes "did not think the film was really bad". Which internal components cause that flip, and is it one head or a distributed circuit?

Question

Which attention heads and MLP layers causally mediate negation's effect on sentiment prediction — and does the effect localize to one component, or spread across several?

Method

  1. Minimal-pair dataset — 960 templated prompts, each ending right before a single-token adjective, so the model's prediction can be read as one clean number: logit_diff = logit(positive_adj) − logit(negative_adj).
  2. Activation patching — patch each of the 144 attention heads and 12 MLP layers from a clean (affirmative) run into a corrupted (negated) run, and measure how much of the effect each one recovers.
  3. Direct logit attribution — an independent, non-patching method: decompose the final prediction additively across every head via the residual stream, to cross-check the first method.
  4. Ablation — zero out the implicated heads and confirm the effect actually breaks, moving from correlation to causation.

Findings

L10H9 is the strongest signal in both independent methods — highest recovery under patching, and the single largest-magnitude score under direct logit attribution. But ablating it alone barely moves the behavior. Ablating the top 10 implicated heads together removes 37% of the effect. The circuit is distributed and redundant, not localized to one head — the same "backup head" pattern documented in GPT-2's IOI circuit (Wang et al., 2022), here reproduced independently for a different behavior.

Agentic AI · Multi-Agent Market Simulation

The Agent Exchange: Specialization Without Assignment

Five identical agents, each with a hidden skill profile they don't know themselves, bid for incoming work in an auction. No agent is ever told what it's good at. Does the market alone route each task to the agent that's actually best at it?

Question

Can a cost-weighted-by-reputation auction produce emergent task specialization from outcome feedback alone — and does it beat naive static routing?

Method

  1. Hidden-skill agents — 5 agents, each with a randomly generated, undisclosed competence vector across 4 task types (code fix, summarization, math, research lookup). The market never sees this vector.
  2. Cost-weighted auction — each agent bids a self-estimated price derived from its own reputation, not its true skill; the winner is chosen by price divided by reputation, so an unproven agent must underbid to earn a chance to build trust.
  3. Asymmetric reputation update — an Elo-style rule where failures move reputation down faster than successes move it up, so the market can't be fooled by a lucky streak.
  4. Static baseline — the identical agents and task stream, routed by round-robin instead of the auction, to isolate what the market mechanism itself contributes.

Findings

By the final 100-task window, the true specialist in each domain wins effectively all of it — code_fix, math and summarization each collapse to a single winning agent, purely from bidding and reputation feedback, with no agent ever told its own skill. The market beat the round-robin baseline 35.7% vs 31.9% success rate. Research lookup — the one task type with no standout specialist — stays contested instead of collapsing, which is itself evidence the market tracks real competence rather than noise.
